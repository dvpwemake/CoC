Photo: New documentary chronicles the late Brazilian artist Amélia Toledo’s boundary-pushing work

She would not stay in one medium

Stones in the water, a search that will not stop

By Dr. Wallace Lynch · Editor in Chief

About 312 words

Amélia Toledo made work from crystals, shells, and stones. She mounted them on steel, set them in water, laid them as mosaics, photographed them magnified. Hélio Goldsztejn's new film, Amelia Toledo: Lembrar de Não Esquecer (Don't Forget to Remember), follows an artist who would not stay in one movement. She was born in São Paulo in 1926 and died in Cotia in 2017.

On Thursday, August 27, 2026, we hold those stones to one pressure: a practice that would not stay put, and a search that will not stop.

In Alpharetta, Georgia, patrolman Dustin Bozzo used Flock 56 times from March to May to look up a former partner, a fellow officer. When her car kept appearing near another officer's, he searched that plate 29 times. After she borrowed her uncle's car, he searched it 18 times. Asked for a reason, he chose Wanted Person or Traffic Infraction. He said it was just a quick keystroke. He resigned August 5. A criminal investigation continues.

Marc Tessier-Lavigne, who left the Stanford presidency in 2023, posted more than 3,000 words on his lab site this week, plus emails with former colleagues. He is answering Theo Baker's book How to Rule the World, which returns to a 2009 Nature paper from his Genentech years and alleges fabricated results and that he hid them.

At Washington State, an AI searched more than 100 million ways to print GRCop-42, NASA's copper rocket alloy. After 40 experiments the system found six that work, one at 500 watts, low enough for common printers. At Ames, new civil servants took the oath inside the 80 by 120 foot wind tunnel, the largest swearing in at the center in nearly 87 years.

Convergence is not a closed file. It is whether the stones can still move, and whether a search that will not stop still has to answer for its reason.
