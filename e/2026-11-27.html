From Orbital Defense to the Corner Booth

Ambition scales up across space, silicon, and cultural institutions.

By Dr. Wallace Lynch · Editor in Chief · ~293 words

Scale is today’s common denominator—whether measured in billions of defense dollars, assembly-line cadence, or the sheer distance between a San Jose diner booth and the upper reaches of global industry.

Nowhere is that expansion clearer than in orbit. A new Novaspace report reveals global governments poured $71.8 billion into space defense and security last year, underscoring an accelerating militarization of the final frontier. Journalist Tim Marshall echoes that sobering trajectory, warning of a “bare-knuckled” geopolitical future above the atmosphere sparked by terrestrial conflicts like the war in Ukraine.

Back on Earth, industrial velocity is hitting a new gear. XPeng is pivoting from electric vehicles to mass-producing its “Iron” humanoid robot, aiming to roll a unit off the line every two and a half minutes. Meanwhile, the hardware of everyday life is getting cheaper and more nostalgic: Black Friday has slashed prices in half on top robot vacuums, and Denny’s has officially restored the San Jose booth plaque honoring Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang following his reinstatement.

In medicine, however, scale meets friction. Even in Boston, America’s putative medical capital, patients face grueling waits for primary care, while impending 2027 Medicare payment cuts have specialists bracing for tightened margins. Simultaneously, the FDA has extended its review of Vertex’s investigational kidney drug amid safety questions, proving that clinical breakthroughs still move at the speed of caution.

Finally, the art world is balancing monumental institution-building with intimate ritual. Hoor Al Qasimi has been tapped to curate the 2028 Venice Biennale, while politicians crowded the aisles of Art Taipei ahead of local elections. Yet in Berlin, artists Ala Roushan and Charles Stankievech used AI to summon a 4,000-year-old archaeological ritual inside Berghain—a reminder that even in an era of massive systems, human culture still thrives in the dark.
