No name on the door

Agents that plan for the unexpected. Three hundred homes that did not ask.

By Dr. Wallace Lynch · Editor in Chief · ~297 words

Danijar Hafner's office in San Francisco's SoMa district sits mostly empty. The startup is in stealth. There is no name on the door. He is building agents that can plan ahead for the unexpected. On the day MIT Technology Review visits, one other person is there.

On Tuesday, September 8, 2026, we hold that empty room to one pressure: who still gets to refuse the plan that arrives without a name.

The housing authority in Concord, New Hampshire, put Minut sensors in three hundred public homes. Noise. Motion. Tenants are suing to take them out. The FDA warned an API supplier about residue on the walls, a corroded product line, and open-toed sandals. Then it named the acting heads of the drug and vaccine centers to permanent jobs. Stability, after the exits. NextEra closed a Department of Energy loan of up to $1.9 billion to restart a shuttered nuclear plant in Iowa. Roanoke's schools are building solar and battery microgrids for ice and heat.

Lucien Smith has died at thirty-seven. Painting, public work, restaurants. An Argentina court ordered a Nazi-looted Old Master portrait returned to the heir of Jacques Goudstikker, from a private collection, through a criminal case. Phaidon will publish more than two hundred works on fungi. Sony is still killing the game disc in January 2028. The last plant is not closing as fast as we thought. Jaden Caradine wanted to be an engineer at eight. He took the Corps, then Embry-Riddle, then NASA. Nature posted an author correction on AhR inhibition and axon regeneration: a stress-growth switch, revised.

An unnamed door. A sensor in the wall. A plant that was dead. Convergence is not the agent that plans ahead. It is whether the unexpected still belongs to the person who lives in the room.
